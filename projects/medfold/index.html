MEDFOLD

A compact folding chair for hospital patient rooms, built on the mechanism of a 1950s school chair.

  • PROD214 group project, 2025
  • Fusion 360
  • Aluminium + polypropylene
  • 1:2 scale model

Drag to rotate · scroll or pinch to zoom

The brief

Design a stable, ergonomic folding chair for hospital patient rooms that's safe, comfortable, easy to clean, compact, light and cheap, using an existing chair's folding mechanism.

Most hospital chairs are big, heavy and expensive, built for comfort over portability. Visitors struggle to find seating near beds, and staff struggle to move and store chairs in tight rooms.

We set targets for a 150 kg load, folding in under 10 seconds, comfortable sitting for up to 2 hours, a 440–470 mm seat height, wipe-clean non-fabric surfaces, and a manufacturing cost under $100 NZD.

Choosing a mechanism

We analysed two existing folding chairs: Chair 12, an unknown 1950s school chair, and the Clarin MS100, a 1970s American event chair. For each one we modelled the mechanism, broke down the parts and manufacturing, and rated its ergonomics against anthropometric data.

Chair 12 was light, had a handle gap and folded flat, but its seat was too narrow (360 mm against a 422 mm average hip width), and the frame dug into your back. The Clarin was comfortable but very heavy, with no handle.

We chose Chair 12's simple 3-bar linkage. Its hinge sits behind the legs when folded, which keeps the folded profile small, and it's simple enough for anyone to use.

Ideas to iterations

Each of the four of us sketched ideas and developed two concepts. Mine explored a simplified mechanism with a calmer look, then a version with armrests, hospital-green upholstery and a caster wheel for moving it when folded.

Concepts 6, 7 and 8 were combined into one updated design. We built a quick prototype to check the mechanism and proportions, and tested seat angles: 5–10° for the seat and 20–30° for the backrest worked best. Steeper angles lifted people's feet and made it harder to stand up, which is a safety issue for patients.

The prototype also showed that the back-leg connector was easy to fit upside down, so it was redesigned to only go one way.

Development

For Assignment 8 we each took developmental chair v1 and made two CAD iterations, eight in total: an "optimised" row refining the updated design, and a "competitor" row aimed at the same market with a different approach. I modelled models 7 and 8. Click "Folded" under any chair to see it fold.

Assignment 6, proposal 6 mine

For Assignment 6 we each designed two folding-chair proposals. This is my second one, with armrests, a handle cut-out, a PE-coated steel frame and caster wheels on the back frame. The group chose it as one of the three proposals (6, 7 and 8) to combine into the next design.

Developmental chair v1

The updated design: a simplified mechanism, streamlined armrests, a backrest with an integrated handle, and crutch-style adjustable legs. This was the "original CAD design" that each of us started from for the eight Assignment 8 iterations.

Model 7, optimised mine

Armrests moved in line with the seat to cut overall width, after user feedback about the wide stance. A smaller back-leg connector that only fits one way, a reinforcing extrusion at the armrest hinge, more hinge-to-seat clearance to stop jamming, and sleeved rod joints for rigidity.

Model 8, competitor mine

A cup holder and cushioned armrest, a backrest cushion joined to the back legs, a crutch slot with Velcro straps, a narrower and more curved seat, and flat feet using an off-the-shelf floor clip.

Final MEDFOLD

Takes the rounded handle from model 1, the armrest position from my model 7, and the combined backrest and leg connector from my model 8, which cut the part count.

The final chair

A hollow aluminium 6061 frame carries injection-moulded polypropylene seat, backrest and armrests. PP was chosen over ABS for being lighter, cheaper, durable and chemically resistant, which is why hospitals tend to use it. The seat cushion is Crypton fabric: liquid-proof, antimicrobial and rated for 10+ years of heavy use.

PP safety hooks lock onto the back legs to stop it tipping forward. The legs adjust in height with a spring-button clip, like crutches, so no tools are needed.
